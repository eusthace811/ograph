Technical explainer

One graph that explains an incident

ograph reads what your operations tools already export, keeps the facts that matter, and answers the questions an outage raises. This page explains how, without assuming you know what a knowledge graph is. Engineers who want the mechanics can go to ograph internals.

  • ograph 0.1.0
  • November 2026
  • 8 minute read
  • Rust, MIT licensed

In short

When a service breaks, the clues sit in a dozen places: the deploy log, the pager, dashboards, chat. ograph reads exports from all of them, boils them down to a few hundred checked facts, and stores those as one connected graph. It then answers four questions: what changed before this, what does it affect, has it happened before, and does any vendor news touch us. It follows the design Netflix described in “Ontology-Driven Observability” and runs as a single command-line program on a laptop or on a corporate network.

1The problem: the answer exists, in pieces

It is late, checkout is slow, and someone has been paged. Almost everything needed to explain the outage is already written down somewhere. A setting was changed eight minutes ago. A database alert fired a minute later. The same thing happened three weeks ago and was fixed by raising a limit.

The trouble is that each clue lives in a different tool, under a slightly different service name, with nothing joining them. A person joins them by hand, under pressure. ograph does the joining ahead of time.

  • What changed before this incident?A timeline of recent events, then a ranked list of likely root causes.
    ograph before ograph chains
  • What does this failure affect?Every service that depends on it, and the business processes behind them.
    ograph blast
  • Has this happened before?Past incidents that looked the same, what caused them and what fixed them.
    ograph similar
  • Does vendor news touch us?News items traced from a vendor to the services that use it.
    ograph news

2A knowledge graph is a pile of short sentences

Forget the jargon for a moment. A knowledge graph stores facts as three-part sentences: a thing, a relationship, and another thing or a value. Because sentences share things, they link up. That linked shape is the graph.

ograph adds a fourth part to every sentence: the file it came from. So any fact can answer the question “says who?”

The ontology is the dictionary

If anyone could write any sentence, the graph would fill with nonsense: a service called pay-gw here and payments-api there, a severity of “bad” in one file and SEV2 in another. An ontology prevents that. It is a short document that lists the kinds of things that may exist, the relationships allowed between them, and the exact words permitted for fields such as severity.

ograph’s ontology is small on purpose. It names applications, teams, regions, business processes and vendors. It names ten kinds of event, such as a deployment, an alert or a burst of error logs. It names incidents, chat threads, candidate causes and human verdicts. It models only what the four questions need.

3Keep the episode, leave the raw data where it is

The obvious way to build this graph is to pour everything in: every log line, every metric point, every trace span. ograph does not. Raw telemetry stays in the systems that already hold it.

What goes in the graph is an episode: one fact that says “this error message came in a burst” or “this metric left its normal range, from here to here”. Each episode keeps a pointer to the first raw record, so you can still drill down.

Smaller is the lesser benefit. The episode graph also gives better answers, because spotting “abnormal” needs statistics that a graph query language cannot express.

4Nothing enters the graph without a check

Readers never write to the graph. They propose facts to a single checkpoint called the gateway, and the gateway decides. A refused fact is not thrown away. It goes to a quarantine area with the reason attached, so you can see what your data is getting wrong.

A model may propose. Only data can confirm.

ograph uses a language model for two narrow jobs: labelling what a chat thread says caused a problem, and spotting dependencies people mention. Both are fenced in.

  • A label must come with a quote from the thread that states the cause. No quote, and the label becomes UNKNOWN.
  • A dependency the model proposes is stored only if the catalog or real traces already show the same link.
  • A name such as inventory-service-uk is not quietly matched to inventory-service-eu, even though the two are 98% alike. A plain misspelling still resolves.

In testing, a deliberately broken model produced invented services, invented quotes and words outside the vocabulary. None of it was stored. ograph also holds no model credentials of its own, and it caches every model answer, so reading the same files twice makes no model calls.

5A worked example: incident INC-5377

The repository ships a small demo company with ten services. Its open incident, INC-5377, is “Checkout latency and payment errors”. Here is the investigation, using real output.

First: what happened just before?

ograph before INC-5377 looks at checkout and everything checkout depends on, and lists the events in the minutes before the incident opened. There are nine.

Second: which of these is the likely cause?

ograph chains INC-5377 links events into chains that follow the dependency graph, scores each chain, and ranks them.

How a link is scored. The rule is the one Netflix published. A pair of events known to cause one another gets a high fixed weight of 0.9. Any other pair is scored on how close in time the two events are and how severe they are: score = time_proximity × 0.6 + severity × 0.4 Chains scoring under 0.6 are dropped. ograph looks back 30 minutes.

Third: how far does it spread?

ograph blast ledger-db walks the dependency links backwards from the failing service.

Fourth: have we seen this before?

ograph similar INC-5377 compares the pattern of events with past incidents and finds one from three weeks earlier.

examples/demo
$ ograph similar INC-5377
  0.599  id:inc/inc-4120 on payments-api at 2026-11-01T22:26:40Z
         causes: []  mitigations: [raised ledger-db connection pool to 200]

The fix that worked last time is right there. The cause list is empty, though, and that is deliberate.

A chain is a guess until a person says otherwise

ograph never promotes its own ranking to a fact. A cause is recorded only when a person confirms it with a verdict, citing their evidence. From then on, the graph remembers.

recording a verdict
$ ograph verdict id:chain/inc-5377-a7370ff7d4358525 accept \
    --evidence PM-5377 --by "jane (oncall)"

6Does it work?

The main test uses RCAEval RE2-OB, a public benchmark of 90 failures injected into an 11-service online shop. Each failure has a known answer. ograph was never told when a fault was injected, and the answer was hidden from it. The score is how often the true root-cause service came first in the ranking.

It is fast because the common questions skip the query language

Any question can be asked in SPARQL, the standard query language for graphs like this. The two questions asked during every incident also have their own purpose-built indexes.

The “what happened before” question takes 0.003 ms, and it took the same 0.003 ms when the graph was made ten times larger. Its index is organised by service and time, so years of history do not slow it down.

7What it does not do

  • It does not decide the cause. It ranks candidates and shows its evidence. A person makes the call.
  • It needs a service catalog, and ogrep to read it. ogrep is a companion code-search tool that already understands Backstage, Compose, Kubernetes and Prometheus files, so ograph asks it for the list of services instead of parsing those formats itself. Without a catalog, every fact about a service is quarantined as unknown and the questions have nothing to answer. Why ogrep.
  • The headline score comes from one benchmark of an 11-service demo system. Six of the 90 cases are still missed, and network faults are harder: 0.80 for packet loss and 0.87 for delay.
  • One ranking rule was written after studying all 90 cases, so the held-back cases are a weaker check for that rule than for the rest.
  • The language-model checks are small. Sixteen chat threads and 21 questions, labelled by the author. They are smoke tests.
  • More signals did not always help. On this benchmark, adding log and trace episodes to metrics brought a little noise.

8Try it in five minutes

Install a prebuilt release on Linux, macOS or Windows (Git Bash), then run the demo.

install, then investigate
$ curl -fsSL https://raw.githubusercontent.com/eusthace811/ograph/main/install.sh | sh

$ cd examples/demo
$ ograph harvest              # read every source, then link events into chains
$ ograph before INC-5377      # what happened just before
$ ograph chains INC-5377      # likely root causes, with evidence
$ ograph similar INC-5377     # past incidents like it, and what fixed them
$ ograph blast ledger-db      # what a ledger-db failure affects
$ ograph why id:inc/inc-5377  # where every fact about a thing came from

Where downloaded programs are not allowed, ./x install builds from source with Rust 1.85 or newer, including behind a private crate registry on a corporate network.

AI agents can use it three ways: a skill for any agent with a shell (./x install-skill), an MCP server with eight tools (ograph serve --mcp), and an extension for pi. The guide covers setting up your own workspace.

Words used on this page

Knowledge graph
Facts stored as linked three-part sentences, so questions can follow the links.
Ontology
The dictionary for the graph: which kinds of things exist, how they may relate, and which words are allowed.
Observability
What your systems tell you about themselves: logs, metrics, traces, alerts.
Episode
One fact that summarises many raw records, such as a burst of one error message.
Gateway
The single checkpoint every proposed fact must pass before it is stored.
Quarantine
Where refused facts are kept, each with the reason it was refused.
Chain
A candidate explanation: a sequence of events leading to an incident. Always a hypothesis.
Verdict
A person’s recorded decision to accept or reject a chain, with their evidence.
Blast radius
Everything that depends on a failing service, directly or indirectly.
SPARQL
The standard query language for knowledge graphs. Roughly, SQL for linked facts.
SHACL
A standard way to write rules such as “every event has exactly one time”.
